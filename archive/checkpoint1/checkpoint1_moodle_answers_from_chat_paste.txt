Answer text Question 4
Two limitations acknowledged by the authors are:

First, GRANDE does not consistently outperform all alternatives on every tabular dataset. The authors themselves state that “there is no ‘one-size-fits-all’ solution for all tabular datasets,” and they present GRANDE as an extension to the existing set of techniques rather than a universal replacement. This is discussed just before Section 4.1, and the results also show that other methods outperform GRANDE on some datasets.

Second, GRANDE has a higher runtime than highly optimized GBDT systems such as XGBoost. In Section 4.2, the authors state that “even though our method is more efficient than previous works on GBDTs such as NODE, GBDTs that leverage GPUs, especially XGBoost, significantly outperform gradient-based methods in terms of run time.”

One limitation that the authors do not explicitly point out is that their empirical evaluation is limited to 19 tabular datasets for binary classification. This matters because the broader claims about gradient-based tree ensembles are therefore not empirically tested on regression or multiclass classification tasks, where their behavior and relative performance could be different.

Answer text Question 5
The target asks us to take advantage of GRANDE’s differentiability by extending it to a setting where gradient-based training provides capabilities that conventional GBDTs do not naturally support; our selected direction is federated GRANDE with gradient aggregation under heterogeneous or non-IID data.

This is the right direction because the main novelty of GRANDE is not just achieving another small improvement in accuracy, but making a tree ensemble differentiable and trainable through end-to-end gradient-based optimization. Traditional GBDTs are already very well optimized for tabular accuracy and runtime, so simply making GRANDE slightly faster or more accurate would provide relatively limited new insight. A federated extension instead makes direct use of what is different about GRANDE and lets us test whether its differentiable parameters can be trained across decentralized and heterogeneous clients while still preserving the useful inductive biases of decision trees.

Class imbalance is one of the properties of the datasets that affects the way the method is trained and evaluated. In fact, some of the datasets in the benchmark contain a relatively small minority class. Answer text Question 9
The paper uses a nested cross-validation approach rather than a fixed train/validation/test split. Final performance is reported as the mean ± standard deviation across 5 outer cross-validation folds, while hyperparameters are optimized with Optuna using 2-fold cross-validation within each outer fold; the authors describe this setup as 5×2 cross-validation.

There is no single separate test set for the whole dataset. Instead, in each of the 5 outer folds, one fold serves as the held-out test data and is kept out of the hyperparameter optimization process.

There is also no fixed validation set. Validation for hyperparameter selection is provided through the inner 2-fold cross-validation, so the outer test fold is not used for tuning. The final reported results therefore come from 5 outer test folds, with no additional repetition of the outer 5-fold procedure.

Question text
Is the split random, stratified, grouped (by patient, site, subject or session), or temporal?

In three or four sentences, explain why that choice matters for this specific dataset, and name one way a wrong choice would leak information between train and test.

Answer text Question 10
The paper uses a shuffled, stratified k-fold split for classification, rather than a grouped or temporal split. In the experiment code, this is implemented using StratifiedKFold(..., shuffle=True, random_state=random_seed). Stratification matters for SpeedDating because only 16.47% of the observations belong to the minority match class, so it helps keep the class proportions similar across folds.

However, each row in SpeedDating represents an interaction between two participants, and the same participant appears in multiple rows because they interact with several different partners during the event. Therefore, a purely row-wise split can place interactions involving the same participant in both training and test folds. This creates a potential form of information leakage because participant-specific characteristics that appear repeatedly across their interactions can be present on both sides of the split; a grouped split by participant or event would be needed to prevent this type of overlap.
